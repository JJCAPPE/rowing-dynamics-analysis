\section*{Research Journal: Video-to-Biomechanics-to-Performance Inference in Rowing}

\subsection*{Purpose of this document}
This document records (i) project decisions and rationale, (ii) progress milestones, (iii) experimental results, and (iv) revisions to the technical plan as the research evolves.

\subsection*{Project objective (scientific statement)}
The objective of this research project is to infer rowing biomechanics and downstream performance-relevant quantities directly from standard (unconstrained) video footage, without requiring instrumented boats or wearable sensors at inference time.

Formally, given single- or multi-view video sequences $V(t)$ of a rowing athlete, the project aims to estimate a temporally consistent 3D human kinematic state $\hat{S}(t)$ and to derive interpretable biomechanical features $\hat{F}(t)$ (e.g., joint angles and segment kinematics) that can be mapped to performance targets $\hat{Y}(t)$ (e.g., force curve, power) via sequence models.

\subsection*{Core pipeline (high-level)}
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Video input} (single- or multi-view; non-lab conditions)
	\item \textbf{Preprocessing} (stabilization, normalization, view/time alignment)
	\item \textbf{2D pose estimation} to obtain keypoints over time
	\item \textbf{2D-to-3D lifting} to recover a temporally consistent 3D skeleton
	\item \textbf{Kinematic and stroke-structure analysis} (biomechanics-first features)
	\item \textbf{Sequence-based inference} from motion features to performance quantities
	\item \textbf{Evaluation} against ground truth (numerical error) and biomechanical plausibility (structural validity)
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Key constraints and principles (design requirements)}
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Unconstrained input:} the primary input is standard video (single camera permitted), potentially with variable lighting, background clutter, camera motion, compression artifacts, and occlusion.
	\item \textbf{No instrumentation at inference:} force sensors, ergometer telemetry, or boat telemetry are \emph{not} assumed at inference time; such signals may be used only for supervised training and validation.
	\item \textbf{Interpretability-first:} intermediate representations should be physically interpretable (biomechanics-derived features) rather than purely end-to-end black-box predictions.
	\item \textbf{Generalization:} models should generalize across athletes, sessions, and filming conditions.
	\item \textbf{Dual evaluation:} accuracy is assessed both numerically (error vs. ground-truth analytics) and structurally (kinematics should obey rowing biomechanics and realistic timing).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Converting spatial resolution $\rightarrow$ required sample rate (RP3 force curve).}
As a first-principles guide, we can convert the RP3 force-curve spatial step into an equivalent sampling frequency during the drive. Using representative values:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Drive time: $\approx 0.7$~s
	\item Drive length: $\approx 130$--$150$~cm
	\item Force curve step: $2.2$~cm
\end{itemize}
The number of force samples per drive is therefore approximately:
\[
	\frac{130}{2.2} \approx 59 \quad \text{to} \quad \frac{150}{2.2} \approx 68.
\]
Converting this to an equivalent sampling rate over the drive:
\[
	\frac{59}{0.7} \approx 84.4~\text{Hz} \quad \text{to} \quad \frac{68}{0.7} \approx 97.4~\text{Hz}.
\]
So, at this spatial step, the RP3 force curve corresponds to roughly $85$--$100$ samples per second during the drive.

\paragraph{Sensible video frame rate.}
If the goal is to align joint-angle sequences cleanly with the force samples without heavy interpolation, the video frame rate should be comparable to (or higher than) the $85$--$100$~Hz effective sampling rate implied above.

\paragraph{Practical recommendation.}
A sensible default target is \textbf{120~fps}: it exceeds the $85$--$100$~Hz requirement and provides buffer for catch timing accuracy, pose jitter, and small timing misalignment.

\paragraph{Empirical comparison (60 vs.\ 120 vs.\ 240 fps).}
In practice, a direct visual comparison supported this choice: $120$~fps looked noticeably smoother than $60$~fps for pose/keypoint trajectories, while the marginal return from $240$~fps was comparatively small.

\subsubsection*{Step 1: Pipeline decomposition and specification (completed)}
\begin{itemize}
	\item Identified the major modules required for end-to-end inference: pose extraction, temporal/kinematic feature computation, stroke segmentation and alignment, and learning-based mapping to performance targets.
	\item Defined the guiding principle that intermediate outputs should be interpretable biomechanical quantities (e.g., joint angles and timing) suitable for scientific analysis.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Step 2: Data extraction; Video to pose time series (in progress)}
\textbf{Goal:} obtain reliable per-frame human pose information for individual athletes from raw video.

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Data ingestion and organization:} collect and index video clips by athlete/session/camera; record metadata (frame rate, resolution, camera viewpoint).
	\item \textbf{Preprocessing:} stabilize video if necessary; normalize scale/rotation when possible; optionally crop to the athlete/boat region to improve pose quality.
	\item \textbf{2D pose estimation:} extract 2D keypoints $\hat{K}_{2D}(t)$ for relevant body landmarks (e.g., shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, ankles).
	\item \textbf{Quality control:} detect and handle missing/low-confidence keypoints; apply temporal smoothing and outlier rejection as needed.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Step 3: Temporal biomechanics feature extraction (in progress)}
\textbf{Goal:} transform pose time series into stroke-relevant kinematic features suitable for analysis and learning.

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Temporal alignment and stroke segmentation:} identify stroke cycles (catch, drive, finish, recovery) using kinematic cues; align cycles to a common phase variable.
	\item \textbf{Kinematic reconstruction:}
	      \begin{itemize}
		      \item Compute joint angles (e.g., hip angle, elbow angle) as functions of time/phase.
		      \item Compute segment velocities and accelerations where meaningful.
		      \item Define task-specific proxies such as estimated handle trajectory/velocity/acceleration (as permitted by the available keypoints and camera viewpoint).
	      \end{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Coordination and timing features:} quantify drive-to-recovery ratio, catch timing consistency, inter-joint coordination patterns, and stroke-to-stroke variability.
	\item \textbf{Canonical representation:} create a standardized feature vector sequence $\hat{F}(t)$ per stroke (or per unit phase) to support downstream modeling.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Step 4: Inference and video--RP3 stroke matching (planned)}
\textbf{Goal:} match each video stroke to its RP3 force stroke, learn a mapping from biomechanics to force-curve shape, and run inference for unseen athletes using video only.

\paragraph{Current technical plan (summary).}
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Define the supervised stroke contract.} Use one stroke as one sample on normalized drive progress $s \in [0,1]$: inputs are active-side joint/trunk kinematics and coordination features; targets are RP3 force values on the same $s$ grid (direct bins and/or PCA coefficients).
	\item \textbf{Pair sessions and stroke indices.} Pair each video session with the correct RP3 export, recover absolute stroke-number correspondence, and reject windows where one-to-one mapping drifts.
	\item \textbf{Canonicalize biomechanics.} Assign \texttt{active\_side} per session, map left/right columns to canonical feature names, and mirror-normalize sign conventions so features are comparable across camera sides.
	\item \textbf{Align in the drive domain.} Segment catch$\rightarrow$finish in video, convert RP3 distance bins to normalized progress, and interpolate video-derived features onto the RP3 progress grid.
	\item \textbf{Apply smoothing and derivative policy.} Keep angle smoothing light, compute derivatives in a stable way, and remove strokes with implausible spikes or non-monotonic progress.
	\item \textbf{Model in two stages.} Stage A: interpretable baselines (linear/tree) on summary features and PCA targets. Stage B: sequence model (TCN/Transformer) for full per-stroke force-curve prediction with masked losses.
	\item \textbf{Evaluate and deploy for video-only inference.} Evaluate by athlete-held-out splits and force-curve metrics (RMSE/MAE/correlation, peak force/position, impulse), then deploy inference that outputs predicted per-stroke force curves with confidence/QC flags.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Issues and risks by step (summary).}
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Data volume (global blocker):} current paired data are too limited/unclear for athlete-level generalization claims; leave-one-athlete-out evaluation requires substantially more athletes, sessions, and strokes across intensities.
	\item \textbf{Pairing/synchronization:} no shared clock and boundary mismatch between video events and RP3 segmentation make this the hardest engineering step; explicit synchronization protocol and acceptance tests are still needed.
	\item \textbf{Drive-domain alignment bias:} RP3 is in physical cm, while video displacement is pixel-space and perspective-distorted; naive normalization can warp kinematics relative to force unless calibrated or flexibly warped.
	\item \textbf{Single-side tradeoff:} active-side standardization improves robustness to occlusion but discards asymmetry information that may explain force differences and provide a quality signal.
	\item \textbf{Derivative instability:} $d\theta/ds$ and especially $d^2\theta/ds^2$ can amplify tracking noise because $s$ is estimated; derivative design and smoothing must be validated empirically.
	\item \textbf{Baseline gap:} a scalar baseline (e.g., stroke rate + stroke length only) is required to quantify how much value biomechanics features add.
	\item \textbf{Anthropometric uncertainty:} true anthropometrics are not directly available from monocular MotionBERT scale; relative segment ratios should be treated as proxies, not calibrated body measurements.
	\item \textbf{Loss/QC sensitivity:} strong smoothness penalties can collapse predictions to average curves; post-pairing QC should include explicit video--RP3 stroke-rate consistency checks.
\end{itemize}


\subsubsection*{Progress update (January 21- February 4, 2026): data extraction experiments and findings}
This section records what has been implemented and learned so far in the video-to-pose portion of the pipeline.

\paragraph{Pose extraction models evaluated.}
For 2D pose extraction, I focused on two strong, widely used options:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{OpenMMLab MMPose} (pose estimation toolbox; used as the main integration framework).
	\item \textbf{RTMPose} models within the MMPose ecosystem (real-time multi-person pose estimation).
\end{itemize}
I also surveyed \textbf{Ultralytics YOLOv8-Pose} models as an alternative family of modern pose extractors.

\paragraph{Initial extraction: 2D keypoints from rowing video.}
My first attempt used straightforward per-frame 2D keypoint extraction on rowing video. Using MMPose, the results were reasonably precise in clear single-person views, but performance degraded under occlusion, and temporal smoothness was sometimes missing (visible frame-to-frame jitter). I experimented with temporal smoothing as a post-processing step; this helped somewhat but did not fully resolve the issue.

\paragraph{Camera motion and stabilization.}
A major issue in real footage was camera shake and inconsistent framing (the athlete was not consistently centered). I addressed this by stabilizing the video via anchoring/tracking a rigid visual reference on the boat (the rigger). After stabilization, I cropped the video so that the athlete remained approximately centered.

\paragraph{Multi-athlete identity switching and tracking attempt.}
Stabilization and cropping alone did not prevent occasional switches where the pose estimator would ``jump'' between athletes (especially in larger boats). To mitigate this, I attempted explicit single-person tracking using \textbf{DeepSORT} so that only one athlete identity would be followed over time. In practice, this approach was ineffective in my setting (frequent failures / unstable identity assignment), so I deprioritized it and moved on.

\paragraph{Qualitative examples (single-person success vs. multi-person failure).}
Figure~\ref{fig:with-elbows} illustrates a representative case where a single athlete is clearly visible; in this regime, pose extraction worked well and individual strokes could be identified reliably from the resulting kinematic traces.

Figure~\ref{fig:bad} illustrates a failure mode: the camera was oriented obliquely (roughly along the boat, rather than approximately perpendicular), causing multiple athletes to overlap in image space and increasing the probability of identity mixing. In this setting, strict ID tracking did not work reliably, and the resulting extracted pose/kinematic data were poor even for very short clips (on the order of two strokes). Because this branch of the pipeline also incurred a large runtime cost (several minutes per short clip in my initial implementation), I discontinued this approach.

\paragraph{Runtime bottleneck.}
Although the overall pipeline (stabilization $\rightarrow$ 2D pose $\rightarrow$ 3D lifting) produced good qualitative results, it was extremely slow end-to-end in my initial implementation (e.g., approximately five minutes for processing a clip containing only a couple of strokes). This is a key practical limitation to address going forward (e.g., model selection, batching, acceleration, and/or reducing redundant computation).

\paragraph{Journal note (February 4, 2026): aligning video kinematics with telemetry domains.}
In on-water telemetry, a common representation is force plotted against the gate/oar angle, whereas in video we naturally observe motion as a function of time. A core challenge for this project is therefore domain alignment: either (i) reparameterize video-derived kinematics from time $t$ into a gate-angle (or other stroke-phase) domain, or (ii) convert telemetry from gate-angle into time. Both directions are nontrivial; my current intuition is to focus on re-indexing video frames into a gate-angle (or stroke-phase) domain so that small increments of gate-angle can be matched to corresponding parts of the force curve. Of course, perspective effects (viewpoint, foreshortening, and occlusion) make this difficult in real boat footage.

As a first ``proof ground'' for this idea, I am considering ergometer video, especially RP3 (a dynamic ergometer) because it provides high-quality force curves and naturally reports force versus distance (stroke length). Distance is plausibly easier to extract from video than gate-angle in the boat setting: by tracking the handle and identifying its start/finish positions, I can segment a stroke by handle displacement rather than by time, and then attempt to align force-versus-distance with video-derived motion. This is still challenging, but it seems like a more tractable first step toward demonstrating viability and research value. Practically, this also fits current constraints: the Charles is frozen right now, so collecting new on-water video is not straightforward.

\paragraph{Pivot to an integrated sports-focused pipeline (Sports2D + MotionBERT).}
After implementing stabilization, tracking, and related preprocessing myself, I concluded that continued iteration on a bespoke pipeline was not the best use of time: it requires extensive tuning and evaluation, and the slow runtime makes experimental iteration costly.

I therefore evaluated open-source pipelines designed specifically for sports/biomechanics use. Many options either required multi-view camera setups (lab-grade markerless motion capture) or were not optimized for sports footage. I selected \textbf{Sports2D} as the most practical alternative: it provides an integrated, configurable pipeline for 2D pose extraction (and associated filtering/tracking utilities) and outputs dense keypoint trajectories that are suitable for downstream biomechanics.

I implemented a new pipeline in which Sports2D provides the 2D keypoints, and I then perform 3D lifting via MotionBERT. A priority in this integration was to preserve as much of Sports2D's pose point data as possible (rather than reducing to a minimal subset), in order to support more fine-grained kinematic feature engineering downstream.

\paragraph{Angle features and stroke event identification.}
With the extracted poses, I computed joint angles of interest (notably elbow, hip, and knee angles). I then plotted these time series (via Matplotlib) to infer stroke events, focusing on detecting the \emph{catch} and \emph{finish} positions as prerequisites for determining the drive phase.

Empirically, once I identified the relevant peaks/troughs in the angle traces, event detection became reliable:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Catch:} occurs at maximal knee compression (knee angle minimum).
	\item \textbf{Finish:} occurs at maximal elbow compression (elbow angle minimum).
\end{itemize}
This heuristic was very effective on the videos tested, and it provides a concrete path toward robust stroke segmentation using kinematics-first signals.

\subsubsection*{Step 3.5: Stroke-length domain tracking and time$\leftrightarrow$distance reparameterization (planned)}
\textbf{Goal:} represent and analyze strokes not only in the time domain $t$, but also in a stroke-length (distance) domain, and enable switching between these parameterizations.

Time-indexed kinematics are natural from video, but many performance signals (and coaching interpretations) are more naturally expressed as functions of stroke progression (e.g., force vs. distance, or force vs. gate-angle on-water). To support direct alignment and comparison, the pipeline should estimate a monotonic ``stroke-length'' coordinate $s(t)$ and provide mappings $t \mapsto s$ and $s \mapsto t$.

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Handle proxy from 2D pose:} estimate the handle position in the image as the midpoint of the left and right hands (or wrists) from the 2D keypoints.
	\item \textbf{Machine motion tracking:} track the moving ergometer component (e.g., carriage/seat/flywheel reference, depending on the machine) to obtain a second motion reference in the same video.
	\item \textbf{Relative stroke-length estimate:} define the stroke-length coordinate as a relative displacement signal, computed from the position difference between the hand-midpoint proxy and the tracked machine motion reference.
	\item \textbf{Domain switching:} use the estimated $s(t)$ to resample kinematics from uniform time steps to uniform stroke-length steps (and vice versa), enabling subsequent analysis in whichever domain is most appropriate.
\end{itemize}

\end{itemize}
Therefore, each $\texttt{curve\_data}$ value corresponds to one force sample at a fixed $2.2$~cm distance step (x-axis = stroke length, not time which is fine thanks to the previous handle tracking we did).

\paragraph{Resolving drive finish overestimation (February 25, 2026).}
The diagnostic viewer from Figure~\ref{fig:stroke-matching} revealed a systematic problem: the video-inferred drive finish was consistently \emph{later} than the RP3-reported drive finish, by roughly $0.38$\,s on average (${\sim}1.02$\,s video vs.\ ${\sim}0.65$\,s RP3). The root cause is that the original finish detection used the \emph{position maximum} of the smoothed handle signal (\texttt{np.argmax}), but the handle continues to travel forward after the rower stops producing force (momentum/coast phase). The RP3, in contrast, cuts off when chain force drops below a threshold, ending the drive earlier. This ``dead zone'' compressed the normalized drive-progress mapping $s_{\mathrm{video}}$ and diluted pose features at high progress values.

\paragraph{Fix A: velocity-based finish detection.}
The handle velocity (already computed as the gradient of the smoothed handle signal) was leveraged to define a better finish point. Within each catch-to-catch segment, the algorithm now: (1) finds the peak handle velocity during the drive, (2) scans forward from the peak to find the first index where velocity drops below a fraction $f$ of the peak (controlled by \texttt{--finish-velocity-frac}), and (3) uses that index as the finish. If the threshold is never crossed, it falls back to the position maximum. This fix is independent of RP3 data and improves standalone video processing, the drive-phase overlay video, and the DP matching scores.

\paragraph{Fix B: RP3 drive-time override for segment export.}
When RP3 data are available, the segment export (\texttt{\_build\_force\_pose\_segments}) can now override the video finish with $t_{\mathrm{finish}} = t_{\mathrm{catch}} + \Delta t_{\mathrm{rp3\_drive}}$, using the RP3 drive duration as ground truth. This is controlled by \texttt{--use-rp3-finish} (enabled by default when RP3 matching is active). It guarantees exact temporal alignment between the video pose window and the RP3 force curve, regardless of residual video detection error.

\paragraph{Tuning and results.}
Using the \texttt{giacomo-6strk} test run, I swept the velocity fraction parameter $f$ and compared the resulting video drive durations against RP3 ground truth (5 matched strokes). Table~\ref{tab:velocity-tuning} summarizes the results. The original position-maximum method ($f = 0$) had a mean absolute drive error of $0.378$\,s. At $f = 0.85$---where the finish is placed when velocity has decayed to 15\% of its peak---the mean absolute error dropped to $0.029$\,s with a slight positive bias of $+0.020$\,s (only one stroke marginally negative). This represents a 92\% reduction in alignment error.

\paragraph{Assessment and audit update (February 28, 2026): implementation plan now in progress.}
Following the latest audit, we confirmed that the dominant remaining mismatch is not a single bug but a stack of interacting timing/design issues in the stroke-signal and inference path. The highest-severity findings were:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Double smoothing:} \texttt{stroke\_signal.csv} currently stores an already-smoothed signal (25-frame rolling mean at \texttt{smooth\_window\_s=0.2}), and the inference path smooths again (11-frame moving average at \texttt{smooth\_window\_s=0.08}), yielding roughly 36 frames of cascaded averaging at 120\,FPS and blunted catch/finish transitions.
	\item \textbf{Dual detector inconsistency:} \texttt{stroke\_signal.py} and \texttt{inference\_cli.py} use different event detectors and disagree substantially (including failed detections and large finish-time disagreement where both detect).
	\item \textbf{Finish mismatch source:} velocity-threshold finish can be placed 28--46 frames before position-maximum finish in some runs, creating a primary visual mismatch channel.
	\item \textbf{RP3 cumulative drift:} long-piece matching can accumulate large timing error (e.g., multi-second drift), indicating DP tolerances are too permissive without a hard cumulative-error gate.
	\item \textbf{LK tracking drift:} machine-reference drift contributes noise but appears secondary after relative-signal cancellation.
	\item \textbf{Butterworth delay:} Pose2Sim uses zero-phase \texttt{filtfilt}, so no filter-induced timing shift is introduced.
	\item \textbf{Axis sign / wrist midpoint / frame timing:} not primary causes in the audited runs.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Fixes implemented}
The implementation is proceeding in four coordinated fixes:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Fix 1: eliminate double smoothing.} In \texttt{\_build\_stroke\_dataframe()}, store raw projection as \texttt{relative\_axis\_px}; keep explicit \texttt{relative\_axis\_px\_smooth} for columns that require smoothing.
	\item \textbf{Fix 2: unify detector path.} Move detection logic into a shared module (\texttt{sports2d\_app/drive\_detection.py}) and make both \texttt{stroke\_signal.py} and \texttt{inference\_cli.py} call the same detector, with one controlled smoothing pass.
	\item \textbf{Fix 3: expose finish method.} Add a clean \texttt{finish\_method} switch (\texttt{velocity\_threshold} vs.\ \texttt{position\_max}) in the shared detector and expose corresponding CLI flags for side-by-side diagnostics.
	\item \textbf{Fix 4: hard-fail cumulative drift.} Add \texttt{--max-abs-cum-error-s} as a hard DP gate, tighten cumulative-growth defaults, and surface per-stroke cumulative error in summary output.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Velocity-calibrated catch/finish detection (February 28, 2026).}
The residual mismatch in Figure~\ref{fig:matching-3}---finish appearing too early and catch too early---motivated a different approach. Instead of fixing a single velocity fraction by hand, we now \emph{calibrate} both catch and finish triggers per run by minimizing the mean absolute error (MAE) of video drive duration versus RP3 drive duration across matched strokes.

The idea: if the RP3 force curve and video handle motion share similar physical triggers (force onset/offset correlate with handle velocity crossing thresholds), then the velocity fractions that minimize drive-duration error should yield the correct alignment. We express both catch and finish as fractions of the per-stroke peak handle velocity: catch fires when velocity rises above $\alpha \cdot v_{\mathrm{peak}}$, finish when it drops below $\beta \cdot v_{\mathrm{peak}}$.

\paragraph{Two-pass flow and calibration.}
When RP3 data are available, the inference CLI runs a two-pass flow:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Pass 1:} coarse detection (zero-crossing catch, velocity-threshold finish) to obtain stroke boundaries; run RP3 matching to get video--RP3 stroke alignment and extract $(\texttt{video\_stroke\_idx} \mapsto \texttt{rp3\_drive\_s})$.
	\item \textbf{Calibrate:} sweep $(\alpha, \beta)$ over a grid (e.g.\ $\alpha \in [0.30, 0.60]$, $\beta \in [0.65, 0.85]$) and compute video drive durations for each pair; choose the pair that minimizes MAE vs.\ RP3 drive durations.
	\item \textbf{Pass 2:} re-detect with $\texttt{finish\_method}=\texttt{velocity\_calibrated}$ and the calibrated fractions; re-run RP3 matching; export segments.
\end{enumerate}
When RP3 is not available, we fall back to empirically optimal defaults ($\alpha = 0.43$, $\beta = 0.75$).

\paragraph{Results.}
On the 10-minute run (\texttt{giacomo-10m\_20260228\_215432}), calibration converged to $\alpha \approx 0.43$, $\beta \approx 0.75$, with MAE $= 15.2$\,ms (down from $\sim$58\,ms with the previous velocity-threshold-only setup) and mean signed error $= -0.3$\,ms. The plateau in the error surface is broad (e.g.\ $\alpha \in [0.40, 0.48]$, $\beta \in [0.73, 0.76]$), so the result is physically grounded rather than overfitted. The calibrated catch shifts later (median $\sim$58\,ms) and the finish shifts later (median $\sim$33\,ms), addressing the visual complaints that the drive phase was too short. Smoothing is reduced to 0.04\,s (5 frames at 120\,fps) for velocity-calibrated mode to avoid blunting velocity peaks. A 25\%-of-cycle clamp on catch shift prevents outlier strokes (e.g.\ the first stroke in a run) from disrupting RP3 matching.
